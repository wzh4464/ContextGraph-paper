\begin{abstract}
Coding agents can repeat costly debugging mistakes even when useful lessons
exist in other repositories. Reusing this experience requires preserving
transferable decisions, connecting related lessons, and retrieving them
when they can guide the next action. We propose ContextGraph, an experience
graph for self-improving coding agents. It distills strategies and warnings
together with their applicability conditions and intended outcomes, retaining
links to the source experience. The agent selects guidance using its full
context before planning and searches again after errors to revise its
diagnosis. After each task, successful and failed trajectories contribute new
experience that subsequent tasks can immediately retrieve. ContextGraph
achieves resolution rates of 69.2\%, 35.1\%, and 72.1\% on SWE-bench
Verified, SWE-ContextBench Related-Lite, and Datacurve DeepSWE, improving
over no memory by 7.6, 13.7, and 11.3 percentage points, respectively, and
leads the evaluated methods on all three benchmarks. Every initial memory
pool in the main comparison is audited to exclude the evaluated tasks. With Gemini 3.1 Pro,
ContextGraph raises Verified pass@1 from 82.3\% to 88.9\%. Ablations show
that abstraction and agent-directed selection over the graph account for most
of the gain, and that the benefit of online updates concentrates in later
tasks of the stream. Most selected lessons come from other repositories,
and cross-repository experience alone accounts for about half of the gain on
two of the three benchmarks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\begingroup
\setlength{\parindent}{1em}
\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
Each repair produces experience in locating faults, revising code, and
checking behavior. Memory reuses this experience on later tasks, offering a
practical route toward recursive self-improvement (RSI)
\citep{godelagent2025,dgm2026}. Both successful and failed attempts contribute.

Recent agent-memory methods retain reflections and repair lessons
\citep{reflexion2023,expel2024} and organize this experience for reuse
\citep{reasoningbank2026,ace2026}. To transfer across repositories, a lesson
must guide investigation in unfamiliar code and identify approaches to avoid.
The agent then determines the local change. Section~\ref{sec:motivation}
illustrates this transfer to Django from an earlier setup failure and a
successful debugging attempt in two other repositories.

We therefore ask: \emph{How can coding-agent memory preserve the conditions
behind a useful decision and help an agent adapt that decision to unfamiliar code?}
Our central idea is to remember when a lesson applies and what behavior it
should preserve, so the agent can adapt it to the current task.

Building this memory involves three challenges. First, a lesson must retain
the useful decision and its applicability conditions while abstracting away
repository-specific names and code. Second, related experiences are scattered
across repositories: similar words can describe different problems, while
different errors can call for the same strategy. Retrieval needs these
connections. Third, planning needs direction for investigation, while errors
bring evidence that can change the diagnosis. Memory must inform both
decisions, then retain the completed attempt for later tasks.

To address these challenges, we propose \textbf{ContextGraph}, a memory graph
that turns experience across repositories into guidance for new repairs
(Figure~\ref{fig:experience-memory}). \emph{Experience abstraction} extracts
strategies and warnings: what to do, what to avoid, and when each lesson
applies. The graph links them to source experiences, problems, and errors.
\emph{Agent-directed selection} uses the full task context to search the
experience pool and follow graph connections, selecting useful strategies
across repositories. \emph{Memory access}
emphasizes two points: before planning and after an error. The first query
uses the available task context; the second adds the error and recent attempt
to guide diagnosis and revision. The agent applies these lessons through
local edits and tests. After every task, ContextGraph distills the successful
or failed trajectory and updates the graph, making new experience immediately
available to subsequent tasks.

ContextGraph reaches 69.2\% resolution on SWE-bench Verified and 72.1\% on
DeepSWE113, exceeding the strongest evaluated memory baselines by 2.2 and
5.9 percentage points, respectively. On Related-Lite99,
its mean resolution rate is 35.1\%, 13.7 points above no memory and 3.8
points above the strongest baseline (Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark-comparison}).
With Gemini 3.1 Pro and live-SWE-agent, it raises Verified pass@1 from
82.3\% to 88.9\%. Every initial pool of the main comparison is audited so that no evaluated
task or near-duplicate fix can be selected (Appendix~\ref{sec:overlap-audit}).

\noindent The contributions are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item We propose ContextGraph, a memory graph for reusing coding experience
across repositories. It connects abstract strategies and warnings to the
problems, errors, and outcomes that explain when the guidance is useful.
\item We integrate memory retrieval into planning and error recovery.
The full task context guides memory selection; observed failures guide
subsequent searches. Successful and failed trajectories update the graph after each
task, connecting current repair decisions to continuing experience accumulation.
\item ContextGraph leads the evaluated methods on SWE-bench Verified,
Related-Lite99, and DeepSWE113, improving Related-Lite99 by 13.7 points over
no memory. Ablations show that abstraction and agent-directed selection are necessary
for any gain and that graph links, online updates, and error-time search add
to it; along the task stream, the benefit of online updates concentrates in
later tasks. Most selected lessons come from other repositories, and they
alone account for about half of the gain on two of the three benchmarks.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
\label{sec:recorded-results}
We ask whether ContextGraph improves repair success, which components
contribute, how the online loop changes later tasks, how much selected
experience crosses repositories, and how the benefits vary across models and
memory-pool sizes.

\subsection{Setup and evaluation}
\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Tasks and metrics.}
We evaluate all 500 SWE-bench Verified tasks, the 99-task Related-Lite subset
of SWE-ContextBench, and the 113-task Datacurve DeepSWE benchmark
\citep{swebench2024,swecontext2026,deepswe2026}. We additionally evaluate
20 Python tasks from LoLBench \citep{lolbench2026}. A task is resolved when
its patch passes the benchmark's official evaluation. Resolution is the
percentage of tasks resolved; pass@$k$ reports success within $k$ attempts.
Related-Lite99 and DeepSWE113 in the main comparison report means over five
runs, each reset to the same initial memory pool.
Table~\ref{tab:evaluation-settings} gives the agents, models, and initial
memory sources \citep{minisweagent2025,sweagent2024}.

\paragraph{Data separation and feedback.}
Initial memory excludes the evaluated tasks' trajectories and reference
solutions. The Nebius SWE-bench-extra trajectories that seed the Verified500
pool contain none of the 500 evaluated instances, and the same instance-level
and patch-similarity audit is applied to every pool of the main comparison
(Appendix~\ref{sec:overlap-audit}). Related-Lite99 follows the
SWE-ContextBench protocol \citep{swecontext2026}: memory starts from a
cold-start pool of SWE-bench trajectories and grows task by task. Tasks enter
the evaluation stream in issue creation-time order. Memory updates use only
agent-visible actions and observations, including its own test runs. Official
test results are used for scoring only: they are neither shown to the agent
nor written into memory, including between repeated attempts.
Code, extraction prompts, graph schema, initial pools, and evaluation scripts
are released in an anonymous repository.\footnote{\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/ContextGraph-XXXX}}
% AUTHOR: replace the placeholder URL with the anonymous repository link.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We compare no memory, FAISS Flat, ExpeL, ExpeRepair, ReasoningBank,
ACE, and Supermemory \citep{expel2024,experepair2026,
reasoningbank2026,ace2026,supermemoryreport2026}. FAISS Flat uses vector
similarity to retrieve memory without graph traversal. We adapt ExpeRepair
and ACE to mini-SWE-agent, retaining their memory procedures. Supplementary
model, pool-size, and retry comparisons state their own inputs and budgets
alongside the results. We compare complete memory systems, including their
construction, selection, and updates. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:main-results-overview}
lists all main comparison values.

\subsection{Repair effectiveness}
\label{sec:benchmark}
\label{sec:recorded-effectiveness}
Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark-comparison} compares eight methods. ContextGraph

On Related-Lite99, its 35.1\% mean improves on no memory, FAISS Flat, and

\textbf{Finding:} ContextGraph improves repair success on all three

\paragraph{Benefits with Gemini and live-SWE-agent.}
With Gemini 3.1 Pro and live-SWE-agent, ContextGraph raises Verified500

\paragraph{Long-horizon development.}
With Codex and GPT-5.5, we build the graph from other Python tasks and evaluate
20 LoLBench tasks. All six memory baselines match no memory at
5.0\% pass@1; ContextGraph reaches 15.0\% pass@1 and 25.0\% pass@3
(Appendix Table~\ref{tab:new-codex55}). With 20 tasks, a difference of two
resolved tasks is not statistically significant, so we report this subset as
a long-horizon sanity check rather than a headline result.

\subsection{Which components contribute?}
\label{sec:ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} removes one component at a time on Related-Lite99
under the main protocol. \emph{Fixed top-$k$} replaces agent-directed
selection with the $k$ most similar lessons by embedding. \emph{No graph
links} keeps agent-directed search but over a flat pool of lessons without
the relations in Equation~\ref{eq:experience-graph}. \emph{Raw trajectories}
skips abstraction and stores trajectory excerpts instead of strategies and
warnings. \emph{Planning-time only} disables the search after errors.
\emph{Static pool} disables updates during evaluation. \emph{Static playbook}
places the most frequently selected lessons in the system prompt of every
task without memory access, testing whether the gain comes from generic
advice. Each variant keeps the remaining components unchanged.

Every component contributes, but not equally. Replacing agent-directed
selection with fixed top-$k$ retrieval costs 10.8 points, the largest drop.
Raw trajectories and the static playbook fall below no memory (20.5 and 20.8
versus 21.4): unabstracted traces and generic advice do not help and can
mislead. Removing graph links costs 4.0 points, freezing the pool 6.4, and
disabling the search after errors 1.9.
\textbf{Finding:} abstraction and agent-directed selection are necessary for
any gain; graph links, online updates, and error-time search each add to it.

\subsection{Self-evolution along the task stream}
\label{sec:stream}
\label{sec:retries}
Memory changes during evaluation. Every completed task is distilled and
becomes available to the next task, so the pool at the end of a run holds
lessons from all earlier tasks in that run, and a retry of an unresolved task
can select the lessons distilled from its own earlier failure. Two views
expose this loop.

\paragraph{Retry passes.}
A separate Related-Lite99 experiment uses DeepSeek V4 Pro with 500 calls and
\$30 per task in both conditions. The initial memory contains 291 curated
repair records with reference fixes, verification hints, and history preceding
the target code version. Each pass is a repair round; later passes retry only
unresolved tasks, and each pass starts from the memory updated by all previous
passes, including the distilled failures of the tasks being retried.
In the run of Table~\ref{tab:swectx-stream}, memory adds 7, 9, and 11
resolved tasks after one, two, and three passes (21, 25, and 27 versus 14,
16, and 16 of 99; five-run means in Table~\ref{tab:stream-position}):
the no-memory agent gains nothing from its third pass, whereas the agent with
memory keeps converting earlier failures into repairs. Excluding ten targets
with near-duplicate source fixes retains the first-pass gain, 19/89 versus
13/89 ($p=.031$). Absolute rates differ from the main comparison because this
experiment uses a different budget and a single run; the paired comparison
within each row is the quantity of interest. Its curated records include
reference fixes, so it tests the update loop rather than the abstraction; the
static-pool ablation (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) shows the same effect under
the main protocol.
\textbf{Finding:} the gap between the two agents widens with each pass, by
7, 9, and 11 tasks; the static-pool control in
Table~\ref{tab:stream-position} attributes the widening to the lessons
added between passes.

\paragraph{Position in the stream.}
If the online loop matters, tasks late in a run should benefit more than
early ones, and the extra benefit should disappear when the pool is frozen.
Table~\ref{tab:stream-position} tests this in the retry experiment with an
additional static-pool arm, tasks arriving in issue creation-time order.
Without memory, each third of the stream yields the same number of first-pass
repairs (4.6, 4.8, and 4.8 of 33), and a static pool adds a roughly constant
amount (5.6, 6.2, and 5.6). With online updates, the first third matches the
static pool (5.4 versus 5.6), while the last third resolves 8.6 tasks against
5.6 with the static pool and 4.8 without memory. Cumulative pass@$k$ shows
the same separation widening with each pass: after three passes, online
updates resolve 26.6 tasks, the static pool 20.2, and no memory 16.2.
\textbf{Finding:} the benefit of online updates concentrates in later tasks
and later passes, the signature of memory that improves through use rather
than of a better initial pool.

\subsection{Cross-repository transfer}
\label{sec:cross-repo}
The Django example in Section~\ref{sec:motivation} drew on two other
repositories. To measure how often this happens, we log the source repository
of every lesson the agent selects during the main comparison.
Table~\ref{tab:cross-repo} reports the share of selected lessons whose source
repository differs from the target repository, and the resolution rate when
lessons from the target repository are excluded at selection time, including
lessons added online. Most selected lessons come from other repositories:
78.4\% on Verified500, 86.2\% on Related-Lite99, and 64.1\% on DeepSWE113.
The DeepSWE113 initial pool contains only LoLBench repositories, none of
which is a DeepSWE113 repository (Appendix~\ref{sec:overlap-audit}), so its
same-repository share comes entirely from lessons added online by earlier
DeepSWE tasks. With same-repository lessons excluded, ContextGraph still
improves over no memory by 1.4, 7.3, and 6.7 points; the full pool adds a
further 6.2, 6.4, and 4.6 points.
\textbf{Finding:} cross-repository experience alone accounts for about half
of the gain on Related-Lite99 and DeepSWE113 and a smaller part on
Verified500; the minority of same-repository lessons contributes
disproportionately.

\section{Conclusion}
ContextGraph remembers when a lesson applies and what behavior it should
preserve, linking transferable strategies and warnings to their source
experience. Agents use their full context to select guidance before planning
and after errors, then contribute new experience after each task. With
DeepSeek V4 Pro, it achieves 69.2\%, 35.1\%, and 72.1\% resolution on
Verified500, Related-Lite99, and DeepSWE113, leading the evaluated methods on
each, and Gemini 3.1 Pro reaches 88.9\% pass@1 on Verified500. Ablations
attribute the gain to abstraction and agent-directed selection, the benefit
of online updates grows along the task stream, and most selected lessons
come from repositories other than the target.
These results establish experience graphs as a strong starting point for
self-improving coding-agent memory.

\section*{Limitations}
The main comparisons measure full memory systems; the ablation in
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} separates their components on one benchmark, the
task-stream study uses the retry protocol with a separate curated pool and
budget, and the cross-repository analysis covers the main protocol only.
The 20-task LoLBench study covers one language and a small part of that
benchmark. Longer task streams and additional agent scaffolds would clarify
when each design contributes most. Detailed settings and additional results
accompany the appendices.

\section*{Reproducibility statement}
The anonymous repository (Section~\ref{sec:setup}) releases the code,
extraction prompts, graph schema, initial memory pools, evaluation scripts,
and the scripts and data that generate the figures
(\texttt{figures/scripts}).
The main text defines the memory workflow, benchmark settings, and reported
metrics. The appendices provide the overlap audit of every initial memory
pool of the main comparison, numerical comparisons, and supplementary
